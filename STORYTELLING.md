# The Story Algorithm

This is a method for taking a simple idea all the way to a finished story in any form: a screenplay, a novel, a TV series, a short story. The steps never change. What changes is what you feed into them, so two people following it faithfully will end up with completely different stories.

It handles unusual stories without special exceptions for one reason. Every traditional writing rule is really a shortcut for an effect on the audience. "Give your hero a clear goal" really means "give the audience something to root for." Great stories break a rule when they find a better way to cause the same effect. So every step and check below is written in terms of what the audience experiences, never in terms of what features a story should have. A story that breaks convention passes the checks as long as it still does the job, and fails them when it doesn't.

## The inputs

Three things go in:

- **The seed:** any starting idea, however small.
- **The audience:** who the story is for and what experience they're coming for.
- **The form:** the medium and the length.

What comes out is a finished story that has been tested on real people.

## The engine: what a story does to an audience

At any moment while someone reads or watches, their mind holds a few things:

- what they know
- what they're wondering
- who they care about, and what they hope or fear for them
- what they expect to happen next
- what they're starting to think the story is about

A story is a planned series of changes to that state. Writing a story means designing those changes.

Four forces pull an audience through a story:

- **Curiosity** is wanting to know: what happens next, what really happened, who this person is, how this works.
- **Concern** is wanting an outcome for someone. That covers hope, fear, dread, longing, and even wanting to see someone get what's coming to them.
- **Delight** is enjoying the moment for its own sake: a joke, a clever move, a spectacle, a surprising idea, someone doing something brilliantly.
- **Meaning** is the sense that the events add up to something true about life. It builds slowly, and it's what the audience takes home. As it builds, it strengthens the other three, because the events start to feel like they matter.

Underneath all four sits one condition: **belief**. The moment the audience stops accepting what's happening as real on the story's own terms, all four forces collapse at once. Belief isn't the same as realism. A story about dragons can have complete belief, and a story about accountants can lose it in one scene.

Everything in the method serves three laws.

**The first law: the pull never stops.** At every moment, at least one force is actively pulling. Different stretches can run on different forces. A quiet scene can run on curiosity and delight while concern rests.

**The second law: pressure builds.** Across the story, the forces grow stronger overall. The questions matter more, the audience cares more, and more is at risk. Dips are fine, even necessary, because they make the rises feel bigger.

**The third law: every debt gets paid.** Every force you build is a debt, because the audience has spent time, attention, and emotional risk holding it. The payoff has to be worth more than what they spent. It can do one of three things:

- deliver what was built
- turn it into something better than they expected
- deliberately hold it back in a way that itself means something

The one outcome that can't be paid for is the audience feeling cheated or forgotten.

## What the most-loved stories share

Look across the biggest audience-voted lists[^1][^2][^3]: The Shawshank Redemption, The Godfather, The Dark Knight, Breaking Bad, The Lord of the Rings, Harry Potter, The Hunger Games, Pride and Prejudice, To Kill a Mockingbird, and others. The same patterns keep showing up.

**The central question is simple and the body is rich.** Will Frodo destroy the Ring? Will Katniss survive? Will Andy get out? A child could state the question, while the details that fill it are deep.

**The premise contains a contradiction.** A mild chemistry teacher becomes a drug lord. The son who wanted no part of the family business ends up running it. The smallest, least warlike people carry the most dangerous object in the world. A war profiteer saves more than a thousand lives. The contradiction produces story on its own.

**The main character is defined early by a costly choice.** Katniss volunteers to die in her sister's place. Andy risks being thrown off a roof to offer a guard financial advice, and asks only for beers for his coworkers in return. One choice tells you who a person is faster than any description could.

**A value is put on trial, and the whole cast argues it.** In Shawshank, Andy stands for hope, Brooks shows what happens when a man loses it, and Red is caught between them. In The Dark Knight, Batman stands for order, the Joker for chaos, and Harvey Dent is the good man who breaks.

**Change is visible and happens step by step.** Michael Corleone, Walter White, and Oskar Schindler each change completely, one choice at a time. When the main character doesn't change, they change the people around them. Andy changes Red, and Atticus changes Scout.

**The climax is a choice, not just a fight.** Batman takes the blame for Dent's crimes. Katniss holds out the poison berries. Frodo fails to destroy the Ring, and the mercy he showed Gollum earlier is what saves the world.

**Details planted early pay off late.** Some reveals change the meaning of everything before them: Andy's rock hammer and poster, Snape's true loyalty, "I am your father."

**The world's rules create pressure.** Only one tribute can survive the Hunger Games. The Ring corrupts whoever carries it. The prison has its own economy and pecking order.

**A core relationship has its own arc.** Andy and Red. Walt and Jesse. Frodo and Sam. Elizabeth and Darcy. Audiences often remember the relationship longer than the plot.

**Victory costs something.** Frodo saves the Shire but can't live in it anymore. Michael wins everything and loses himself. Katniss survives and is never the same.

**Tones mix.** Dark stories are funny in places, and violent stories are tender in places. The contrast makes each tone hit harder.

**Skill is fun to watch.** Walt's chemistry, Andy's accounting, Katniss's archery, Hermione's knowledge.

Research says the most popular stories tend to have reversals, with fortunes rising and falling, rather than a steady climb or slide, and that stories ending in a fall are among the most popular.[^4] It also says nearly all stories share one basic shape (set the stage, move the plot forward, build tension), but that sticking closely to that shape doesn't make a story more popular.[^5] Structure is the floor, not the ceiling. What lifts a story above the floor is everything else on this list.

Even the greatest stories slip:

- The middle repeats itself.
- A rescue arrives from nowhere.
- A side character exists only to help the hero.
- A theme gets said out loud instead of shown.
- A rule appears right when it's needed.
- A character changes faster than the story has earned.

The final season of Game of Thrones is the best-known recent example of that last one. Its biggest character turns were widely criticized as rushed, because the steps leading to them weren't shown.

The classics hit the patterns above in their best stretches and miss them in others. This method applies the same pressure to every part of the story, on purpose, so the best stretches aren't accidents. No method guarantees a masterpiece, since taste, effort, and many drafts still matter. But a method can make the strengths deliberate and the common failures rare. That's how you beat even great work consistently.

## How to run it

Run the steps in order the first time. Each step produces something you write down, and each ends with a check. If a check fails, fix it there. If you can't, the problem started earlier: go back to the earliest step it traces to, fix it, and re-run everything after it. Thinking is cheap early and expensive late, so the method puts the hard decisions first.

At every decision, generate more options than you need, including the obvious one and its opposite. Then choose using the step's check. The first idea that comes to you is usually the first thing the audience will guess too, which kills surprise. Keep the obvious option only when it beats the others on merit.

If an AI is running this, the same rules apply, with one addition. Write out the result of every step in full before moving to the next. And when you reach the reading steps, read as a stranger who doesn't know the plan.

## Phase 1: From idea to premise

### Step 1: Take the idea apart

Your seed can be anything: a character, a situation, a world, an image, a theme, a twist, an ending, a mix of genres, or just a feeling. Write it down.

Then write down the feeling it gives you, the specific thing that made you want to tell it. "A man discovering his whole life was staged" might feel like vertigo and betrayal. That feeling is the one thing to protect. Every other part of the idea is allowed to change.

Next, note which basic parts of a story the seed already gives you and which are missing:

- someone to follow
- something they want
- something in the way
- a place
- a reason it matters

The next steps fill the gaps.

**Check:** Can you name the feeling in a few words? If not, the seed isn't ready. Spend more time with it, or choose another.

### Step 2: Turn the idea into many premises

A premise is the story in one sentence: a specific person wants something specific, something makes it hard, and it matters for a reason. Write at least fifteen to twenty versions, changing one thing at a time:

- who the character is
- what they want
- what's in the way
- where and when it happens
- the scale (one family or a whole nation)
- the genre
- whose eyes we see it through

Push each version toward contradiction. Try the person least suited to the situation, the situation that attacks what the person values most, or the goal that can only be reached by betraying something. Make sure the set includes the obvious version, its opposite, and a few that feel risky.

**Check:** Do you have real variety, or fifteen versions of the same premise? If they're all alike, change bigger things.

### Step 3: Choose the premise

Run each candidate through six tests:

- **The stranger test:** Say it in one sentence to someone. Good premises make people ask a question or react.
- **The engine test:** Does it create problems on its own, without you forcing them?
- **The room test:** Can it escalate many times, at your intended length, without repeating itself?
- **The distance test:** Name the most famous story it resembles. If yours would feel like a copy, change a core part until it stands on its own.
- **The feeling test:** Does it still deliver the feeling from step 1?
- **The "only you" test:** Does something in your own knowledge or experience make you the right person to tell it? This one isn't required, but it's the most reliable source of specific, surprising detail.

**Check:** You have one sentence that passes at least the first five tests.

### Step 4: Find the question underneath

Ask what value this premise puts under pressure. Phrase it as a question real people disagree about, with at least two answers a thoughtful person could defend:

- "Is it worth losing yourself to protect your family?"
- "Can hope survive in a place that punishes it?"
- "Is peace worth a lie?"

Avoid questions with an obvious answer, like "Is cruelty bad?" Nobody feels pulled by a question they've already settled.

This question is the theme. The story won't state it. It will test answers to it through what characters do and what it costs them. Decide for now which answer the ending will give: yes, no, yes but at a terrible price, or an open answer where the point is that the question has no clean resolution. You'll revisit this in step 10.

**Check:** Can you make the strongest possible case for the answer you personally disagree with? If you can't, you don't understand the question well enough yet, and the story will turn into a lecture.

### Step 5: Define the promise

Name the main experience the audience is coming for, such as fear, laughter, romance, thrills, a puzzle, awe, grief, triumph, or unease. Add one or two secondary ones. This decides which forces lead. A mystery runs mostly on curiosity, horror on dread, comedy on delight, and romance on concern, but every story needs some of all three. Then choose the form and length.

Finally, list the moments the audience would feel robbed without, given what kind of story this is. A romance needs the first meeting and the moment they choose each other. A thriller needs the final confrontation. A mystery needs the reveal. You'll deliver every item on that list later, either straight but better than usual, or replaced by something that delivers the same feeling more strongly.

**Check:** Can you say in one sentence how the audience should feel when it's over? That sentence is the promise.

## Phase 2: People

### Step 6: Build the main character from the question outward

The main character (or characters) is the person through whom the theme question gets tested. Decide these things:

- **What they want.** Make it concrete and visible, so the audience can tell whether they got it: escape the prison, win the war, win the person they love, pay for treatment. If the want is internal, like being loved or respected, give it an outward form the audience can track.
- **What they believe about the theme question at the start.** One option is that they're wrong, and the story will force them to change or break (Walter White, Michael Corleone, Elizabeth Bennet). The other is that they're right and the world is wrong. Then the story tests whether they'll hold on, and they change the people around them instead (Andy Dufresne, Atticus Finch).
- **Why they believe it.** This is the history or wound behind it.
- **What they're good at.** Skill is enjoyable to watch and gives them ways to act.
- **Their flaw.** It's usually their strength taken too far, like Walt's pride in his genius or Elizabeth's trust in her own judgment.
- **What they'd never do.** The story will push them right up to that line.
- **One early choice that shows all of this at once,** like Katniss volunteering.

The main character doesn't need to be likable. They need to be watchable, meaning the audience wants to spend time with them for some reason:

- sympathy (they've been wronged)
- admiration (courage or skill)
- fascination (they're mysterious or dangerous)
- humor
- recognition (they're like us)

**Check:** Does what they want put them in direct contact with the opposition? Does what they believe make it harder to get? If the want and the belief don't collide, the character's inner life and the plot will run on separate tracks, and the story will feel hollow. Fix it here.

### Step 7: Build the pressure

Decide what makes the outcome uncertain: an opponent, a group, a system, nature, time, the character's own weakness, or the pull of the unknown. Most great stories stack several.

When the pressure is a person, give them their own want, their own answer to the theme question, and a logic they believe in. The strongest opponents are often a dark version of the main character, or embody the answer the main character is tempted by. Gollum is what Frodo could become. At the start, they should be stronger than the main character in the ways that matter, and they should adapt whenever the main character does.

When the pressure is a system, nature, or time, give it faces and moments. These are specific encounters the audience can experience directly, like one particular guard who stands for the whole prison.

**Check:** At the start, does the audience believe the main character could really lose? If the opposition is a person, could you argue their side convincingly?

### Step 8: Build the cast as a system

For every other important character, answer four questions:

- What do they want, apart from the main character?
- What's their answer to the theme question?
- What job do they do for the story? Examples: pushing the main character, tempting them, showing what they could become, giving them someone to protect, or letting the audience see them from outside.
- How does their relationship with the main character change?

Look for mirrors: characters facing the same question who choose differently. Brooks, who can't survive outside prison, shows Red what he's afraid of becoming. Harvey Dent shows how even the best man can be broken.

Choose at least one core relationship and give it its own arc that tracks the main story.

Merge characters who do the same job. Two characters with the same function split the audience's attention and weaken both.

When a story has several leads, give each one the full step 6 treatment, and let the theme question tie them together.

**Check:** If you removed any character, would the story lose something only they provide? Could the audience say what each major character wants?

## Phase 3: World

### Step 9: Build the world as a pressure machine

Define the rules that matter to the conflict:

- what's possible
- what's forbidden
- what things cost
- who holds power, and how power is lost

In a realistic setting, the rules are the real legal, social, physical, work, and money realities. Research them, because specifics are what make an audience believe.

Put limits on every kind of power, whether magic, technology, money, or influence. Limits create problems. Unlimited power dissolves them.

Build at least one rule that makes the theme question harder. In The Hunger Games, only one tribute can survive, which turns "Can you stay human?" into a life-or-death problem.

Give the world more texture than the story strictly needs, so it feels like it keeps going past the edges. But only show what serves the story.

Establish rules before they matter. A rule revealed at the moment it saves someone feels like cheating. The same rule shown earlier for another reason feels clever.

**Check:** Does the world create problems for the characters on its own? Does it make the theme question harder to answer?

## Phase 4: The spine

### Step 10: Design the ending first

Decide the central question the audience will carry the whole way. Usually it's "Will this person get what they want?" It can also be "What really happened?", "Who is this person?", or "What will they become?" Sometimes the audience knows the outcome in advance, as with a true story or an ending shown first. Then the question becomes "How?", "Why?", or "At what cost?"

Design the decisive moment, the climax, where that question gets answered. The strongest climaxes force the main character to choose between two things they value. The choice shows who they've become and gives the story's answer to the theme question.

Write at least five possible endings:

- the expected one
- its opposite
- one where they get what they want but lose what they need
- one where they lose what they want but gain what they need
- one that combines or twists these

Choose the ending that best does all of this:

- answers the central question, or leaves it open in a way that is itself an answer
- answers the theme question through action
- keeps the promise from step 5
- surprises the audience while feeling inevitable afterward

Decide the cost: what's lost even in victory, or gained even in defeat. Endings where nothing is paid feel weightless.

Design the final moment: an image of the new normal that mirrors the opening, so the audience sees the change without being told.

**Check:** If someone saw only the first scene and the last, would they see a meaningful change in the character, the world, or their own understanding?

### Step 11: Work backward and plant what the ending needs

List everything the audience must already know, believe, or feel for the ending to land: skills, objects, rules, relationships, the character's beliefs, earlier failures, promises made. Each one has to appear earlier. That early appearance is a plant: something shown early that matters later.

Disguise each plant by introducing it for a different reason, so it doesn't give the ending away. Andy's rock hammer arrives as a hobby. The poster arrives as a joke.

If the ending includes a reveal that changes the meaning of earlier scenes, list those scenes and make each one work both ways. It should play one way the first time and another way once you know the truth.

**Check:** Could the audience guess the ending from the plants alone? Then disguise them better or add a false trail. Would the audience reject the ending even after seeing every plant? Then plant more, and earlier.

### Step 12: Design the opening

The opening has four jobs:

- **Hook.** Raise a question within the first moments.
- **Show the "before."** Show the main character's normal life, with their belief or flaw visible in something they do.
- **Set up the mirror.** Show the image the final moment will answer.
- **Disrupt.** Something breaks normal life and sets the want in motion.

The disruption should come as soon as the audience can care about it. That means either they already feel something for the character or situation, or the disruption itself is the hook. The "before" can be shown first or revealed later. What matters is that the audience eventually knows who this person was, so they can see who they become.

**Check:** After the opening, does the audience know what to wonder about, whom to watch, and what kind of story this is?

### Step 13: Build the middle as a ladder

This is where most stories fail, usually because the character keeps running into obstacles of the same size.

List every way the main character could try to get what they want, from the easiest and most natural to the most desperate. Each attempt should fail, or succeed at a cost. The result should force the next attempt, by closing a door, revealing something, or creating a new problem. The opposition adapts to each attempt.

Tighten as you climb:

- Options shrink.
- Stakes grow, from the personal to the people they love to everything they value, or deeper into who they are.
- Time runs short.
- Costs get heavier.

Along the way, place three key moments:

- **A turn near the middle** that changes what the story is about. It can be a reveal, a false victory, or a false defeat that flips the direction. The hunted becomes the hunter, or the goal turns out to be the wrong goal.
- **A low point late in the story.** This is the moment the want seems lost, caused at least partly by the character's own flaw or choices.
- **A turn toward the climax.** Something the character realizes, decides, or learns makes the final choice possible.

Plan the character's fortunes as a series of rises and falls, not a steady climb or slide. Longer stories need more reversals, and each should hit harder than the last.

Side stories (subplots) should each test the theme question from another angle and feed into the main line, ideally meeting it at the climax.

**Check:** Is each rung harder than the one before? Does each rung cause the next? Could any two rungs trade places without anything breaking? If so, the link between them is missing. Build it, or cut one.

### Step 14: Plan who knows what, and when

Separate what happens (the events in the order they occur) from how it's told (the order and viewpoint the audience gets).

For every important fact, decide which characters know it and when the audience learns it. You have three tools:

- **The audience knows less than a character:** you get curiosity.
- **The audience knows more than a character:** you get concern. They can see the bomb under the table, and the character can't.
- **No one knows, then everyone learns at once:** you get surprise. It works best when it changes the meaning of what came before.

Choose the viewpoint: whose eyes the story sees through, how closely, how many viewpoints there are, and how far the narrator can be trusted.

Choose the telling order:

- straight through
- told by someone looking back (Red in Shawshank, Death in The Book Thief)
- ending shown first
- several timelines
- scrambled order

Any departure from straight telling has to earn more curiosity, concern, delight, or meaning than it costs in confusion.

Plan background information. Give it when the audience needs it. Ideally that's when they're already asking for it, so it feels like a reward. Or deliver it under pressure, in an argument or a threat. Never let it become a pause.

**Check:** At every point, is any confusion something you chose, and does the audience trust it'll be resolved?

### Step 15: Fit the spine to the form, then break it down

Research says the basic shape holds at any length.[^6] What changes is how much each form can hold:

- **A short story** usually carries one change, told close to the moment it happens, with the ladder compressed to one or two rungs.
- **A feature film** carries one central question and only a few characters with full arcs, and everything has to be visible or audible.
- **A novel** has room for inner life, side stories, and several questions at once. It can carry slower stretches if curiosity and delight hold them up.
- **A TV series** needs two engines, described below.
- **A series of books or films** works like a TV series at a larger scale. Each installment answers its own question while pushing the bigger one forward.
- **An interactive story** lets the player's choices replace some of the main character's. The same checks apply: each choice should be hard and have real consequences.

The first TV engine is a season spine: steps 10 through 13, applied to each season. The second is an episode engine: a repeatable situation that produces a new story every week (a case, a job, a family crisis) while the season spine moves in the background. Each episode needs its own question answered and a new or deeper one left open. Character change has to be spread across seasons, in stages that each feel complete.

Then break the spine into units: large parts, then sequences (groups of scenes chasing one short-term goal), then scenes. Each unit is a small version of the whole story. It opens a question, turns, and leaves the situation changed.

**Check:** Does every unit open a question, turn, and change something?

## Phase 5: Scenes

### Step 16: Design each scene

For every scene, write down:

- **What changes:** the situation, a relationship, what someone knows, or what someone believes. If nothing changes, cut the scene or merge it with another.
- **Who wants what,** right now, and what's in the way.
- **The turn:** the moment the scene's direction shifts, through a reveal, a decision, or an action.
- **The outcome:** better or worse for the character. Vary it. If every scene ends badly, or well, the audience stops feeling it.
- **The audience's state:** which question opens, which one closes, and what they now hope, fear, or expect.
- **Plants and payoffs.**
- **The delight:** what makes this scene enjoyable in itself, like a joke, a clever move, a striking image, or skill on display.

Then put the scenes in order:

- Link them by cause: "because of that" or "but then," never just "and then."
- Vary the intensity. After a big scene, give the audience a quieter one where feelings can land.
- Start each scene as late as possible and leave as soon as it has changed.
- Show the most important moments in full. Summarize or skip the least important ones.

**Check:** Can you state each scene's change in one sentence? Would cutting the scene break something later?

### Step 17: Design the moments inside each scene

Characters go after what they want with tactics like persuading, threatening, charming, lying, and bargaining, and they switch tactics when blocked. That switching is what makes a scene feel alive.

Treat every line of dialogue as an action: someone trying to get something from someone.

Use the gap between what people say and what they mean. People rarely state their feelings or goals directly, and the gap creates both curiosity and delight.

Let characters be smart. Let them make their best move given what they know. Audiences love watching intelligence, and nothing breaks belief faster than someone missing the obvious because the plot needs them to.

When a character states the theme outright, it should be one side of an argument, not the story's verdict.

**Check:** In each exchange, can you say what each character is trying to do to the other?

## Phase 6: Draft

### Step 18: Write the whole draft

Follow the plan, but treat it as a best guess. When writing reveals something better, like a character surprising you or a connection you didn't plan, keep it, note it, and keep going. Don't polish yet, because polishing scenes that may be cut is wasted work.

Finish. A complete rough draft is worth more than half a perfect one, because the next steps only work on a whole story. When you're done, take every discovery you noted back to the earliest step it affects, update that step, and adjust what follows.

**Check:** Is it complete from first moment to last?

## Phase 7: Revision

### Step 19: Read it cold

Take a break long enough to forget the details. Then read or watch it start to finish as a stranger would, without fixing anything. For each scene, note:

- what you're wondering
- whom you care about, and what you hope or fear for them
- what you're enjoying
- what you expect next
- whether you believe it
- where your attention drops, where you're confused without meaning to be, or where you feel cheated

Sketch a rough line of how strongly you're pulled through the story. Flat stretches and sudden drops are your list of problems.

### Step 20: Run the revision passes

Each pass means going through the whole draft asking one question. Fix what fails, then move to the next.

1. **Pull.** Find every stretch where no force is strong. Add a question, raise a threat, add delight, or cut it.
2. **Cause.** Could any scene move elsewhere without breaking anything? Then it isn't connected. Link it by cause or cut it.
3. **Motive.** At every decision, would this person, knowing only what they know, really do this? If they act to serve the plot, change the plot or change what they know.
4. **Intelligence.** When a character misses something the audience can see, does the audience feel dread or frustration? Keep the dread and fix the frustration.
5. **Plants and payoffs.** List them all. Every payoff needs a plant. Every noticeable plant needs a payoff, or a reason to be there.
6. **Luck.** Coincidences that get characters into trouble are free. Coincidences that get them out are debts. Pay them with an earlier plant or a real cost, or remove them.
7. **Stakes.** At every stage, does the audience know what can be lost? Does it grow?
8. **Escalation.** Is each challenge harder than the last? Is the middle repeating itself?
9. **Theme.** Does each major character and side story test the question? Is the answer shown through consequences rather than said? Does the other side get a fair case? A story that argues against a weak version of the other side feels like a sermon.
10. **Ending.** Is it both surprising and inevitable? Does it cost something? Does it answer the central question and the theme question? Does it mirror the opening?
11. **Opening.** Rewrite it now, last, knowing the ending. Add plants, sharpen the mirror, and strengthen the hook.
12. **Clarity.** Is every confusion chosen, and does each come with a sense that an answer is coming?
13. **Expectations.** Find every moment where the audience expects something familiar. For each one, deliver it better than usual, replace it with something that delivers the same feeling more strongly, or cut it. Never twist something just to be different. The replacement has to give more than the familiar version would have.
14. **Specifics.** Replace every generic choice (a generic job, motive, setting, or villain) with a specific one. Specifics create belief and delight at the same time.
15. **Contrast.** Is there emotional range, with humor in dark stretches, tenderness in violent ones, and calm before storms? A single tone numbs the audience.
16. **Economy.** For each character, scene, and side story, ask which force it serves. If none, cut it. If two things do the same job, merge them.
17. **Second viewing.** Does the story reward a second read or watch? Find places where a line, image, or scene could carry a second meaning that only the ending unlocks.
18. **Promise.** Did you deliver the experience promised in step 5, and every must-have moment on your list, either straight or transformed?

### Step 21: Test it on real people

Give it to people like your intended audience. Ask them to mark where they were bored, confused, didn't believe something, or felt something strongly.

At a few points, ask what they think will happen next. If they guess right too often, the story is predictable. If they have no guess at all, it may be unclear, or they may not care yet.

Trust their reactions about where something went wrong. Be skeptical of their ideas about how to fix it. If you use an AI as a test reader, have it report the step 19 notes scene by scene as a first-time reader, and don't tell it what you intended. You want to see whether your intentions come through on their own.

### Step 22: Loop until done

Trace each problem to the earliest step it comes from, fix it there, and re-run the steps after it. Symptoms often appear far from their cause:

- A sagging middle usually traces back to a weak want (step 6) or weak opposition (step 7), not to the middle scenes.
- A flat ending often traces back to a theme question with an obvious answer (step 4).
- A forgettable story often traces back to a premise without a contradiction (step 2).

Stop when all three of these are true:

- A cold read has no dead stretches, no unintended confusion, and no moments of disbelief.
- Test audiences can say what the story was about and felt the ending was earned.
- Your changes have become sideways: different, but not better.

The audience will never see any of this. They'll only feel a story that seems like it couldn't have gone any other way.
